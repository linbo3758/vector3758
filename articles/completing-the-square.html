Completing the “Square”:Rectangle or Power of 2?

Visualisation of Completing the Square's geometric meaning and further applications.

Completing the square is one of the most familiar techniques in GCSE maths, an extremely helpful tool in finding a quadratic’s vertex, or solving an equation.

What exactly is the “square” being completed?

Is it referring to a square number, such as \(x^2\), because quadratics involve powers of 2?

Or is it referring to a geometric square — a shape with four equal sides?

Such a pun! The algebraic form \(a(x+\frac{b}{2a})^2 - \frac{b^2}{4a} + c\) hides a beautiful geometric story. Let’s uncover it step by step.

The word “square” is not only an algebraic operation. It also describes a geometric shape.

Algebra

Geometric understanding and algebraic interpretation are never far apart.

A quadratic equation completed its square: \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\). Now the vertex is \((h,k)\).

Either the parabola is positive or negative, the vertex is the minimum or maximum. The vertex is all worked out when \((x-h)^2=0\), (as the squared term has a known lower bound: \(0\)), therefore \(x=-h\), \(y=k\). Here, completing the square (degree of 2) makes life easier by taking \(x\) as which value to maximise/minimise the whole equation.

Use the exact same example with above, \(x^2+6x=55\). The roots can be worked out by \((x+3)^2 = \sqrt{64}\). Two real roots here, \(5\) and \(-11\). Same as the answer we got from factorising it \((x-5)(x+11)\).

The quadratic formula can be deduced by completing the square:

\[\begin{aligned} & ax^2+bx+c=0, \qquad a\ne 0 \\ & x^2+\frac{b}{a}x+\frac{c}{a}=0 \\ & x^2+\frac{b}{a}x=-\frac{c}{a} \\ & x^2+\frac{b}{a}x+\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = -\frac{c}{a}+\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 \\ & \left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2} \\ & x+\frac{b}{2a} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a} \\ & \boxed{x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}} \end{aligned}\]

Calculus

Vertex, the terminology of maximum/minimum point in a quadratic graph — maximum, minimum point, which is the stationary point in terms of calculus. A point with \(dy/dx=0\) (rate of change = 0, gradient = 0).

\(ax^2+bx+c\)

\(dy/dx = 2ax + b = 0\)

\(x = -b/2a\)

Integration can be understood as the reverse process of differentiation. If differentiating a function gives us its rate of change, integration allows us to work backwards and recover the original function.

For example,

\[\frac{d}{dx}(x^3)=3x^2\]

Therefore,

\[\int 3x^2\,dx = x^3 + C\]

The constant \(C\) is necessary because the derivative of any constant is zero. Indefinite integration therefore gives a family of functions rather than a single function.

For quadratic expressions, completing the square can reveal a useful structure before integration. Consider

\[\int (x^2+6x+13)\,dx\]

Completing the square gives

\[x^2+6x+13=(x+3)^2+4\]

The integral becomes

\[\int \bigl((x+3)^2+4\bigr)\,dx\]

Using the power rule for integration,

\[= \frac{(x+3)^3}{3} + 4x + C\]

Here, completing the square makes the expression easier to recognise and integrate, although expanding the original polynomial and integrating each term would also work.

Its usefulness becomes clearer in more complicated expressions, such as

\[\int \frac{1}{x^2+6x+13}\,dx\]

Completing the square transforms the denominator into

\[(x+3)^2+4\]

This matches the standard integral form involving the inverse tangent function, giving

\[\frac12\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x+3}{2}\right)+C\]

In this case, completing the square does more than simplify the expression: it reveals the mathematical form needed to identify the integral.
